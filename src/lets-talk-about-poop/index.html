Let's talk about poop

A list of names

Every animal poops, but almost none of them get to use the same word for it. English has invented a separate name for nearly every creature's leavings, and naturalists still use most of them with a completely straight face.

The names sort themselves three ways: who made it, what it looks like, and what it's useful for. A few belong to one group of animals and nobody else — guano only ever comes from birds and bats. Others are really just descriptions of shape: a pellet is a pellet, whoever squeezed it out.

Everything here is written for Indian animals and Indian ground, with a bias toward the south. The English names came in with English naturalists, so a few of them describe animals we don't have — where that's true, it's flagged.

The one word that covers everything

Poop — or feces, if you're being formal

If you only want one word, this is it. Poop works for an elephant, an owl, an earthworm, a caterpillar and you. So does its scientific twin, feces (spelled faeces outside the US). Neither assumes anything about the animal's size, diet or shape, which is exactly why scientists reach for feces when they need a word that can't be wrong.

Out in the field, scat is the everyday catch-all that wildlife biologists and trackers use, and droppings is the polite version for dinner-table use. Both are useful, but both quietly lean toward particular animals in practice. Poop and feces never leave anyone out.

Whodunit? Guessing the animal

Here's the thing most guides bury: in any given patch of forest, the list of animals that could have left it is short. Often one or two. Once you know that list, a single clue is frequently enough to settle it. Go in this order — each step narrows the field before the next, and you may well have your answer by step two.

The whole thing, boiled down

You're really only asking four questions

Every clue below is a readout of one of these. Learn the four and you can work out a species you've never read about, instead of memorising a list.

1. What did it eat?
Read from the contents. Hair and bone, or grass fibre, or whole seeds, or fish scales, or shiny beetle cases. This splits meat from plants immediately, and often names the exact meal.
2. How big is the animal?
Read from the width. A gut can only make something as wide as the gut is. Bear in mind that a growing animal spends months producing perfectly convincing smaller-species sizes.
3. How does its gut work?
Read from the form. Neat dry pellets mean a long, thorough, water-saving gut. A sloppy pat means a fast wet one. A hair-bound rope means a short meat-eater's gut. And a white cap on top means one exit rather than two, so birds and reptiles pass their pee in the same parcel.
4. Why did it leave it there?
Read from the placement. Plant eaters drop and keep walking. Meat eaters put it on the path, the junction, the high rock — it's a message. A spot used over and over is a shared noticeboard.

And two questions that aren't about the poop at all

  • Who could possibly be here? The strongest filter of the lot, and it applies before you've looked at anything. It can rule out the other three questions entirely.
  • How long has it been sitting? Weather shrinks it, fades it and changes its texture, so age has to be subtracted before the other four can be trusted.

Every name in the table above is just a nickname for a common combination of these. Dung is big + plants + a front-fermenting gut. Guano is a bird or bat + one exit + a few centuries of accumulation. Frass is a very small animal + a leaf.

  1. 1

    Know the neighbourhood before you get there

    This is the strongest filter you have, and you apply it before you've looked at anything. Wildlife doesn't spread evenly. Krishnagiri is thick with elephants. Nandi Hills, Chikkaballapur, Doddaballapur — an hour or two away — have exactly zero. So a heap of dung on a Nandi trail isn't an interesting puzzle, it's cattle, and no amount of squinting at its shape will make it anything else.

    The same logic settles the big cats. Most of the scrub and forest patches around Bengaluru hold leopards and no tigers at all. In those places a large cat scat is leopard — there's no second candidate to rule out, so you don't need to measure anything. Go to Bandipur or Nagarhole, where both live, and the question genuinely reopens.

    So do the reading first. Forest department checklists, eBird and iNaturalist records for the exact area, camera-trap reports, and — best of all — whatever the local watchers and shepherds tell you. Knowing that a species simply isn't present is worth more than any clue on this page.

  2. 2

    Shape — plant eater, meat eater, or a bit of both?

    This is the big fork in the road. Separate little pellets, or a fibrous pat, means a plant eater. A twisted rope that narrows to a point at one end means a meat eater — that point is hair, binding the tail end together. Loose blobs studded with seeds that change through the year mean an animal eating both, working its way through whatever happens to be fruiting.

  3. 3

    Width, not length

    Length just depends on how long the animal stood still. Width depends on the gut that made it, which is why field guides publish widths. Roughly: jungle cat under 2 cm, leopard 2–3 cm, tiger 3–5 cm. Something the size of your thumb was not left by something the size of a bus.

    But read those numbers as grown-up numbers. Animals start small and pass through every width on the way up, so a half-grown tiger produces scat squarely inside the leopard range. Where both species live, width alone cannot separate them — see below.

  4. 4

    Break it open — what's inside is the receipt

    With a stick, never a finger. Hair and bone chips mean a predator, and the hair often tells you what it caught. Shiny beetle wing-cases: sloth bear, civet, mongoose, plenty of birds. Whole undamaged seeds: a fruit eater, doing the forest a favour by planting them. Coarse grass fibre: a grazer. Fish scales and crab shell: otter or fishing cat.

    Chalky, white and crumbly deserves its own note, because it's easy to over-read. It doesn't name a species — it means a lot of bone went through. Calcium survives digestion, so it comes out the far end as pale powder. The striped hyena — our hyena — is the extreme case, since cracked bone is a staple for it rather than an accident. But any meat eater that's been eating bone can produce it: a leopard or jungle cat that swallowed small prey whole, a wolf, a jackal, and — the version you've almost certainly seen — the village dog that got hold of a bone. Tigers and leopards on large kills strip more meat and leave the big bones, so their scat is usually hairier than it is chalky, but a run of small prey will whiten it.

    And check that the white isn't just age. Old scat of any species bleaches in the sun. The difference: bone-white is pale all the way through and crumbles to powder, while sun-bleached scat is pale on the outside, light as a husk, cracked, and still shows the hair and structure it had when fresh.

  5. 5

    Where it was left, and how deliberately

    Plant eaters drop and walk. Meat eaters make a statement. Poop parked in the middle of a path, at a junction, or on a raised rock is a territory notice — that's cats, the dog family (wolf, jackal, fox) and the otter and marten family. A pile that's clearly been used again and again is a toilet site, or latrine: badgers, civets, otters and rhinos all keep them. Streaks down a trunk or cliff mean a bird roosting above. Buried and scratched over, looking suspiciously domestic? Likely a house cat, which means you're nearer a village than you thought.

  6. 6

    Work out how old it is

    Wet, shiny and covered in flies: a few hours old, and the animal is probably still nearby. Dull, dry and crusted: days. Grey, falling apart, with fungus or beetles moving in: weeks. Age won't tell you the species, but it tells you whether the question is still worth chasing.

  7. 7

    Gather more signs — as many as the shortlist demands

    How much backup you need depends entirely on step 1. If only one animal in the area could have left it, you're already done — go ahead and call it. Where two candidates genuinely overlap, that's when you start hunting for corroboration within a few metres: paw prints in soft ground, scrape marks, claw scratches on bark, flattened grass, the remains of a kill.

    The uncertainty is a property of the place, not of poop. Tiger-or-leopard is a real question in Bandipur and a non-question on a Bengaluru hillock. Wolf-or-jackal-or-village-dog is genuinely hard almost everywhere the three overlap, so that one deserves a print alongside it. Know which situation you're in, and you'll know whether to keep looking or just enjoy the find.

The question everyone asks

Whose hair is in there — the prey's, or the cat's own?

Almost entirely the prey's. A leopard doesn't skin its kill; it eats the animal fur and all. Hair is made of keratin — the same stuff as your fingernails — and no mammal has the equipment to digest it. So it travels the whole length of the gut untouched and comes out the far end, matted together in a bundle. That bundle is what holds the scat in a rope shape and pulls it into a point at one end.

You can tell it isn't the cat's own coat by the sheer volume. A single scat can be more than half hair, which is far more than any animal could swallow off its own body in a day.

The clincher is that researchers routinely work out what a predator has been eating by pulling the hair out of its scat and looking at it under a microscope. Each species' hair has its own pattern of surface scales and its own internal structure, so a strand can be matched against a reference collection: sambar, chital, langur, cattle, hare. That entire method only works because the hair belongs to whatever got eaten.

Cats do swallow their own hair while grooming, so a little of it is genuinely in there too. But it's a small share next to a furry meal — and a good deal of self-groomed hair never reaches the far end anyway. Hairballs come back up the way they went in.

The pairs that fool everyone

Worth saying again: each of these is only a problem where both animals actually live. Cross one off using step 1 and the pair stops being a pair.

Young tiger vs. grown leopard
The hardest pair on this list, and the one where the width numbers betray you. A tiger on its way to adult size passes straight through leopard territory on the tape measure, and both are big cats eating the same kind of prey in the same forests, so the contents look alike too. Big prey — gaur, a full-grown sambar — tilts it toward tiger, but a young tiger that's just left its mother is killing small things, which is exactly when the two overlap most. Your best real-world tie-breaker is a clear paw print beside it. Researchers stopped trying to eyeball this one altogether: where tigers and leopards share a forest, scat is sent for DNA testing, because that's the only answer that holds up.
Dog vs. wolf or jackal
Pet dogs eat packaged food, so theirs is soft and much the same every time. Wild ones are full of hair, bone and whatever fruit is in season — and placed far more pointedly.
Deer vs. goat
The pellets look nearly identical. Judge by the company instead: goats turn up in herds, with hoofprints and usually a herder. Deer pellets sit in scattered clusters under cover.
Leopard vs. striped hyena
Both are hairy and pointed when fresh. Hyena poop goes chalk-white fast and often lands that way, because bone is a staple. Leopard poop usually darkens and shrinks instead — but it whitens too after a spell of small prey eaten whole, so treat white as "ate bone", not as a name.
Civet vs. small cat
Civet poop is full of fruit and seeds in season, and turns up at the same toilet spot again and again. Cats either bury theirs or leave a single deposit on a path.

Before you go poking at it

Poop spreads disease, and this is the one part of the article that isn't a joke. Use a stick and gloves, never bare hands. Meat-eater poop can carry tapeworm eggs. Rat and mouse droppings can carry a nasty virus. Bat and bird guano in a closed cave or attic puffs fungal spores into the air when disturbed, so don't kick the floor about and definitely don't lean in for a sniff. Wash your hands properly afterwards. Photograph it, measure it, move on.

Not poop: the owl pellet

That tidy grey parcel of fur and mouse skulls under an owl's perch came back out of the beak, not the other end. The owl coughed it up. It never got as far as the intestines, so it never became poop at all. Hawks, herons, kingfishers and gulls do the same thing.

So the pellet you pull apart in a classroom is, technically, vomit with an excellent publicist. The owl's actual droppings are the white streaks on the bark above it.

Fun fact

Some plant eaters eat their own poop — deliberately, and only the first batch

A rabbit makes two completely different things. The dry round pellets you find scattered on the grass are finished waste. The other kind are soft, dark and shiny, clumped together like a tiny bunch of grapes and wrapped in a film of mucus. These are cecal pellets — scientists call them cecotropes, and you'll see it spelled caecal in British books.

You almost never see one, because the rabbit eats them the moment they appear, straight from its own bottom, swallowed whole. This isn't desperation or bad manners. It's a deliberate second trip through the body, and without it the animal would slowly starve on a full stomach.

Why? It comes down to plumbing

No mammal can actually digest the tough stuff that plants are built from. Every plant eater borrows the ability from microbes living in its gut, which do the breaking-down on its behalf. The only real question is where along the pipe those microbes live — and that single detail decides whether the animal has to eat its own poop or not.

Cud-chewers — microbes near the start

Cattle, buffalo, gaur, chital, sambar, nilgai, goats, sheep. Their fermenting chamber — the rumen, which is really a first stomach — sits before the intestines. Microbes break down the plant matter, multiply enormously, and then get swept downstream into the intestines, where the animal digests the microbes themselves along with the vitamins they made. Everything valuable is produced upstream of the part that does the absorbing. Nothing gets left behind.

Rabbits & co. — microbes near the end

The Indian hare, pet rabbits and guinea pigs, and a great many rodents. Their fermenting chamber — a pouch called the cecum — sits after the intestines. So all that microbe protein and all those vitamins get made past the exit, brewed up in a room with no door back into the rest of the house. Eating the cecal pellet is how the rabbit posts it back to the start of the line.

So the cow and the rabbit are two answers to the same problem. The cow chews again but never eats again — that's all cud is. The rabbit never chews again but does eat again, once, and only the right batch. Keep a rabbit from reaching its cecal pellets and it will lose condition even with unlimited food in front of it.

Two things people mix up: chewing cud is food coming back up from the front of the gut, not a second helping from the back end. And not every animal that eats poop is doing this — young elephants and koala joeys eat their mother's droppings for an entirely different reason: to pick up the gut microbes they weren't born with. That's installing the software, not running the program twice.

Eight animals you'll actually run into

Each of these is the four questions worked through in advance. Read them as examples of the method, not as a list to memorise — and remember that the giveaway line only counts once step 1 says the animal could be there at all.

Elephant

The easiest poop in India to identify, and the most informative.

Look
Enormous rounded lumps, roughly 15–20 cm across, four or five of them dropped together. Pale brown and openly fibrous — it looks like wet, chopped hay pressed into a ball.
Inside
Barely processed grass, bamboo, bark, palm fibre, whole seeds. An elephant digests less than half of what it eats, so you can more or less read yesterday's menu straight off the pile.
Where
Along trails, forest roads and crop edges. Dropped while walking, so a herd leaves a trail of piles pointing the way it went.
Giveaway
The lump width tracks the animal's size, so a pile of small boluses among big ones means a calf in the herd. Dung beetles find it within the hour — a pile with no beetles yet is very fresh, and you should be paying attention to where you are.

Leopard

The big cat you're most likely to be sharing ground with.

Look
A rope about 2–3 cm thick, in linked segments, narrowing to a twisted point at one end. Dark and shiny when fresh, and pale when it's been sitting.
Inside
Hair, and lots of it, with bone chips and the odd hoof fragment. Chital and langur in forest; near towns and plantations it's very often dog, plus goat and calf. The hair is the meal's, not the cat's.
Where
Deliberately public: the middle of a path, a track junction, a culvert mouth, a flat rock. It's a message, so it goes where other leopards will walk.
Giveaway
Hair-bound rope on an open path, with a scrape mark beside it. In most of peninsular India there's no second big cat to confuse it with — which is the whole argument for knowing your patch.

Sloth bear

Changes completely with the season — the same animal leaves two different-looking things.

Look
Big, loose and shapeless rather than rope-like — a dark splattered mass. Much sloppier than a cat's, and several times the volume.
Inside
In the dry months, termites and ants by the thousand — so packed with insect shell that a fresh pile can actually glint in the light — mixed with grit swallowed while digging into mounds. In fruit season it flips: ber, jamun, figs and mahua, sometimes almost nothing but pulp and whole seeds. Ants and termites are the year-round fallback and turn up in scat in most months, but "turns up in" and "makes up most of" are two different claims, and fruit wins the second one whenever it's available.
Where
Rocky scrub and dry deciduous forest, on trails and near the boulder piles they shelter in. Look for wrecked termite mounds and claw-scored rock nearby.
Giveaway
Nothing else its size is that full of insects. A civet's poop is also seedy and insect-flecked, but it's a fraction of the volume.
Bonus
Identify the seeds and you've learnt something better than the bear's identity: you know which trees within its walking range are fruiting right now, and therefore where it has been eating and will come back to. A seed that doesn't match the season is its own clue — either the pile is older than it looks, or the bear is ranging further than you assumed.

Monkeys — langur and bonnet macaque

Identified as much by the pattern on the ground as by the poop itself.

Look
Soft irregular lumps or short blunt sausages, dark brown to greenish. They've usually fallen a long way, so they hit the ground flattened and scattered rather than in a neat pile.
Inside
Langurs eat leaves, so theirs is fibrous and green-tinged. Bonnet macaques eat everything, so theirs is full of fruit pulp and seeds — and around temples, bus stands and homestays, human food, right down to shreds of plastic.
Where
Directly under sleeping trees, and it builds up over weeks because troops reuse the same ones. Also along compound walls, temple roofs and water tanks.
Giveaway
Scattered across a wide patch under one big tree, with chewed-off leaves and dropped half-eaten fruit in the same spot. Poop that fell out of the sky has a look of its own.

Indian hare

The animal from the cecal pellet story, above.

Look
Round pellets around a centimetre across, slightly flattened, dry and light. Pale to mid brown, and they crumble into coarse plant fibre between your fingers.
Inside
Nothing but chopped grass and leaf fibre. Practically odourless — all the goodness was extracted on the second pass.
Where
Scattered singly along field bunds, cart tracks and scrub edges, dropped while feeding at night rather than left in a heap.
Giveaway
Goat pellets are the lookalike, and the tell is company, not shape: goats come as a herd with hoofprints and usually a herder, and leave dense piles. Hare pellets turn up one or two at a time with nothing else around.

Indian crested porcupine

Best identified by what's lying around it.

Look
Stubby elongated pellets, bigger than a hare's, sometimes several strung together by the plant fibre running through them. Very light, very fibrous.
Inside
Coarse root, tuber and bark fibre — a diet of hard, dug-up plant matter rather than soft leaves.
Where
Around burrow mouths in rocky scrub, and along the paths they use night after night between the burrow and a crop field.
Giveaway
Dropped quills, and gnawed bones. Porcupines chew old bones and antler for the calcium, so a scatter of bones scored with neat parallel tooth marks near a burrow is as good as a signature.

Indian grey mongoose

A small meat eater that keeps a fixed toilet.

Look
A miniature version of the carnivore pattern: a thin twisted rope under 1.5 cm thick, tapering at one end, often curled around itself.
Inside
A jumble: beetle wing-cases and other insect shell, rodent hair and tiny bones, reptile scales, crab shell, eggshell flakes. Mongooses eat whatever they can catch, and it shows.
Where
Returned to the same spot repeatedly — a rock slab, a culvert, the base of a wall, a gap in a stone bund — so you find a collection of different ages together.
Giveaway
Small, insect-heavy, and piled at a reused site. One thin twisted rope on its own is hard to call; a dozen of them on one rock slab is a mongoose latrine.

Indian peafowl

A big bird leaves a big, unmistakably bird-shaped dropping.

Look
A thick coiled or curled sausage, olive to brown. Fresh ones carry a chalky white cap or streak — that white is the pee, arriving in the same parcel, the one-exit design — but don't expect it every time. On a grain eater the white is a small share of a bulky dropping, and a day of sun and dust turns it dull grey and blends it in. It shows up boldly on a rock or a leaf; it disappears into dry ground.
Inside
Grain, seeds, grit and insect fragments — a bird that spends its day walking through fields and scrub, eating both.
Where
Heaped under roost trees, because they sleep in the same ones and let go overnight. Otherwise scattered along field edges, village outskirts and temple grounds.
Giveaway
Size, and the setting. It reads as chicken droppings scaled up, and there'll be scratched-up dust bowls and a dropped feather or two nearby to settle it. Don't lean on the white cap — see below.
Catch
Peafowl are chicken relatives, and like chickens they have a pair of large fermenting pouches — the same cecum the rabbit uses. Birds don't eat the contents; they simply void them every so often as a separate cecal dropping: soft, tan, sticky, evil-smelling and with no white cap whatsoever. So if peafowl droppings often look nothing like the textbook picture, that's why — a fair share of them are a different product altogether.

The other three bears

India has four bears, and they barely overlap. That's the geography filter doing your work for you: if you know where you're standing, you almost always know which bear you could be looking at before you look at anything.

Asiatic black bear

The Himalayan bear — foothills to treeline, and the one most likely to be near a village.

Look
Large loose piles, often a flattened cowpat shape rather than anything rope-like. Colour swings wildly with the season — black in berry months, pale yellow-brown on maize.
Inside
Overwhelmingly plants. Acorns and oak mast in autumn, walnuts, wild fruit and berries, bamboo and green shoots in spring — and, near settlements, a great deal of maize and orchard fruit. Meat is occasional, so hair is the exception here rather than the rule.
Where
Himalayan and north-eastern forest, roughly the foothills up to the treeline, moving higher in summer and dropping into valleys and crop fields in autumn.
Giveaway
Look up. These bears climb to feed and haul branches in towards themselves, leaving a wad of broken, dead branches wedged in the fork of an oak or fruit tree — a "bear nest". Add claw scars on smooth bark, and a big vegetarian pile underneath, and you're done.

Himalayan brown bear

Above the trees — alpine meadows of Ladakh, Kashmir, Himachal and Uttarakhand. Rare, and worth reporting when you find sign.

Look
Big and loose. In summer it's often startlingly green and grassy, closer to a small cow's than a predator's.
Inside
Alpine grasses and herbs, roots and tubers, and — the interesting part — marmot. Fur and small bones in a big high-altitude pile is a strong hint.
Where
Open alpine meadow and scree well above the treeline, in summer. Also, unfortunately, around army camps and rubbish dumps, which is its own conservation problem.
Giveaway
Excavation. A brown bear digging a marmot out leaves a crater — turned earth and flung rocks around a burrow mouth, far more violent than anything a marmot does to its own front door.

Sun bear

India's smallest and least-seen bear — the far north-east only.

Look
Smaller and more compact than the other three, but still loose and shapeless rather than formed.
Inside
Figs and forest fruit, termites and ants, beetle grubs, honey and bee brood. Insect-heavy like a sloth bear's, but in wet tropical forest rather than dry scrub.
Where
Arunachal, Nagaland, Mizoram, Manipur and Assam's hill forests — nowhere else in India. If you're not in the north-east, it isn't a sun bear.
Giveaway
Torn-open rotting logs and ripped bark, worked over with very long claws for grubs and honey — plus the geography, which does most of the work.

The bear shortcut: sloth bear in the dry scrub and deciduous forest of the peninsula and central India; Asiatic black bear through the Himalaya and north-east; brown bear above the treeline in the western Himalaya; sun bear only in the north-east. Where two do overlap — black and brown bear in the western Himalaya — altitude usually separates them, forest against open meadow.

Up in the hills

The mountains add a second filter to step 1, and it's a sharp one: altitude. Species sort themselves into bands, so gaining a thousand metres swaps the cast list as thoroughly as driving to another state. Knowing roughly how high you are cuts the candidates faster than any clue in the poop itself.

Foothills and lower valleys

  • Barking deer (muntjac) — small dark pellets, often clumped rather than perfectly separate, dropped singly in dense cover along stream banks. Usually you hear the animal before you find anything.

  • Rhesus macaque — the northern counterpart of the bonnet macaque, and read exactly the same way: scattered under a reused sleeping tree, full of fruit, and full of human food anywhere near a town.

  • Yellow-throated marten — a twisted little rope, dark and often crammed with fruit and seeds, left pointedly on a rock, log or the middle of a forest road. Bold, frequently seen in daylight, and a serious fruit eater despite being a predator.

  • Wild boar — irregular soft lumps, sometimes loosely joined, full of roots and mast. The real sign is the ground: rooted-up soil in patches, as though someone went at it with a hoe.

  • Leopard — same animal, same reading as in the south, and it goes remarkably high. Don't assume the hills put you out of leopard country.

Middle hills and forest

  • Goral and serow — dry dark pellets on cliff ledges, grassy spurs and steep broken ground. The two are hard to separate by pellet alone; use the ground instead, since goral favours open grassy crags and serow prefers thick forested slopes.

  • Musk deer — small pellets, and the notable thing is that they're deposited at reused latrine sites rather than scattered. A repeatedly used pile of small pellets in high conifer or birch forest is worth a careful look — this is a heavily poached animal.

  • Himalayan pheasants — monal, koklass, kalij — bird droppings with the usual pale cap, but the better sign is the digging: monals turn over soil and leaf litter for roots and grubs, leaving distinctive small pits across a slope.

  • Red fox — a small twisted rope with a hair point, parked on something prominent: a boulder, a tussock, a path junction. Often stuffed with fruit and beetles as much as with rodent.

Above the treeline — alpine, Ladakh and Spiti

  • Pika — tiny round pellets in quantity around rock crevices and boulder fields. The unmissable companion sign is the haypile: a stash of cut plants left out to dry for winter, tucked under a rock. Nothing else up there does that.

  • Marmot — larger droppings, concentrated at toilet spots beside burrow mouths, on open meadow. The burrow system, worn paths and a sentry whistling at you make the ID before the poop does.

  • Blue sheep (bharal), ibex, urial — pellets, in quantity, on ledges and bedding sites. Nearly impossible to tell apart from each other or from domestic sheep and goat by pellet alone. Location and altitude decide it — and see the warning below.

  • Snow leopard — a hair-packed rope like any big cat's, but the placement is the signature. It goes on ridge crests, at cliff bases and along the narrow travel routes they favour, very often beside a scrape — a patch of ground raked backwards by the hind feet. Bharal hair inside. Find scrapes and scat together on a ridgeline and you've found a marking site, not a random deposit.

  • Wolf — bigger than a fox's, thick with hair and bone, and left conspicuously at trail junctions. In Ladakh and Spiti, livestock hair in it is common, which is exactly the friction that gets wolves killed.

  • Kiang — the Ladakh wild ass, which like other horses builds up dung piles at repeatedly used spots. Big coarse grassy boluses heaped together on open plains, with no village or herder anywhere near them.

The mountain warning: most of it is livestock

High pastures are grazed hard through the summer. Sheep, goats, horses, donkeys, cattle, yak and dzo are all up there, and their droppings vastly outnumber everything wild. Before getting excited about a pile of pellets on an alpine meadow, look for the boring explanation first: hoofprints in numbers, a trampled route, a stone corral, dung collected and stacked for fuel. Wild ungulates leave their pellets on steep broken ground and ledges that a herd doesn't bother with. And in a Ladakhi or Spitian village, dried yak and cow dung is not sign at all — it's the winter fuel supply.

Worth noticing what this vocabulary is for. English built its fine distinctions around hunting — telling one quarry from another. Indian languages built theirs around farming: which dung fuels a fire, which plasters a floor, which goes on the field. Different obsessions, same instinct to give every kind its own name.

What the shikar years left behind

Medieval English had a gloriously fussy set of hunting words — fewmets for deer, crotels for hare, lesses for boar and bear. None of it reached India. Those words were already museum pieces by 1800, and the sportsmen who wrote the shikar books here never used them: read Sanderson, Forsyth, Corbett or Anderson and you get plain "droppings" and "dung", every time.

What the period did leave is a small working vocabulary that Indian field naturalists still use daily — and the traffic ran mostly the other way.

Pugmark
A paw print. From Hindi pag, a footstep, welded onto English mark — a word coined in India that went home to English rather than arriving from it. It's now standard technical usage: for decades India counted its tigers by tracing pugmarks onto glass, before camera traps and scat DNA took over. The one piece of vocabulary on this page you'll hear in a Forest Department office tomorrow morning.
Spoor
Everything an animal leaves as it goes — tracks, droppings, scrapes, hair. Dutch by way of South African hunting, and imported into Indian sporting writing wholesale. Old-fashioned now, but you'll meet it constantly in the second-hand shikar shelf.
Shikari
The local tracker every one of those Englishmen depended on completely. Worth saying plainly: the skill of reading dung and pugmarks in India was Indian. The British-era contribution was writing it down in English and putting their own names on the covers.

Words for the shape, not the animal

These describe what it looks like and where it's sitting, so they work across all sorts of animals. They're the words field guides lean on most.

  • Pat — a flat splat that spread out on landing. Cattle, gaur, buffalo. Means a wet diet.

  • Bolus — one packed lump that kept its shape. Elephant, rhino, horse.

  • Pellet — small, separate, dropped by the dozen. A fibrous diet, and an animal good at squeezing the water back out before it leaves.

  • Latrine (or midden) — a place, not a shape. Badgers, otters, rhinos and civets come back to the same toilet spot for years. It works as a noticeboard.

  • Sign — a tracker's word for every trace an animal leaves behind. Poop is sign; so are prints, scrapes and rub marks.

Why bother with any of this

Because poop is the most generous thing a wild animal leaves behind. It tells you what lives here, what it ate last night, how healthy it is and whether it's breeding. The gut sheds a few of its own cells into every deposit, so there's even enough DNA in there to identify the exact individual. India's tiger numbers lean heavily on this: scat collected across the reserves, typed in a lab, counted — without anyone seeing, drugging or disturbing a single tiger.

The animal walked off hours ago. The information stayed behind. That's a fair trade.
